# Finance Glossary — fin-health Dashboard Knowledge Base

This glossary defines the financial terms and metrics present in the
fin-health dataset.  Use these definitions when users ask about the
meaning, calculation, or interpretation of any metric.

---

## Profitability Metrics

### Net Profit Margin
- **Definition:** The percentage of revenue remaining after all expenses,
  taxes, and costs have been deducted.
- **Formula:** Net Income / Revenue × 100
- **Interpretation:** Higher margins indicate better cost control and
  pricing power.  A negative margin means the company is losing money.
- **Healthy range:** 10 %–20 % for most industries; tech companies often
  exceed 20 %.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −50 % to +35 %.
- **Industry context — why "good" margins vary dramatically by sector:**
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** Industry average ~20–30 %. Software
    has near-zero marginal cost, so mature tech firms enjoy very high
    margins. A margin below 10 % for a large IT company would be
    concerning.
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Industry average ~2–5 %. Logistics is
    a high-volume, low-margin business — Amazon historically reinvested
    nearly all profit into growth. A 3 % margin here is healthy; expecting
    20 % would be unrealistic for this business model.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** Industry average ~15–25 % for franchise-
    heavy models. McDonald's earns high margins because franchisees bear
    most operating costs; the company collects royalties. Non-franchise
    restaurants typically see 5–10 %.
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** Industry average ~15–25 %. Banks
    earn spread income (interest received minus interest paid), and
    margins depend on the interest-rate environment. Low rates compress
    bank margins.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Industry average ~15–30 %.
    High R&D costs but strong pricing power for leading-edge chips.
    NVDA has had margins above 30 % due to GPU dominance; INTC has
    compressed margins from competitive pressure.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Industry average ~5–10 %.
    Regulated utilities have capped returns, so thin margins are
    structural, not a sign of poor management.
  - **FINTECH (PYPL):** Industry average ~10–20 %. Transaction fees
    provide good margins but payment processing is competitive.
  - **FINANCE (SHLDQ):** Sears had persistently negative margins in its
    later years due to declining retail sales — a sign of terminal
    business decline, not sector norms.

### Return on Equity (ROE)
- **Definition:** Measures how effectively a company uses shareholder
  equity to generate profit.
- **Formula:** Net Income / Shareholder Equity × 100
- **Interpretation:** Higher ROE signals efficient use of equity capital.
  Very high ROE (> 50 %) may indicate high leverage rather than
  operational excellence.
- **Healthy range:** 15 %–25 %.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −80 % to +160 %.
- **Industry context:**
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** Industry average ~25–40 %.
    High margins and asset-light models drive strong ROE. AAPL's ROE
    can exceed 100 % because aggressive buybacks have shrunk equity,
    inflating the ratio — this is leverage-driven, not purely
    operational.
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** Industry average ~8–15 %.
    Banks have massive balance sheets, so even solid profits produce
    modest ROE. Post-2008 regulations also require banks to hold more
    equity, further compressing ROE.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Industry average ~15–30 %.
    NVDA has had exceptionally high ROE during GPU/AI booms. INTC's
    ROE has declined with market-share losses.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** ROE can be extremely high or negative
    when equity is near zero or negative from buybacks. Interpret with
    caution — check if equity is negative before reading ROE.
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Industry average ~10–20 %. Heavy
    reinvestment keeps equity high, moderating ROE.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Industry average ~8–12 %.
    Regulated returns cap profitability.
  - **Key insight:** When ROE is very high (> 50 %), always check the
    Debt/Equity ratio. High ROE + High D/E = returns driven by
    leverage, which is riskier than high ROE + low D/E (genuine
    operational excellence).

### Return on Assets (ROA)
- **Definition:** Indicates how efficiently a company uses its total
  assets to generate profit.
- **Formula:** Net Income / Total Assets × 100
- **Interpretation:** Higher ROA means better asset utilisation.
  Capital-intensive industries typically have lower ROA.
- **Healthy range:** 5 %–15 %.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −15 % to +30 %.
- **Industry context — why ROA varies so widely:**
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** Industry average ~15–25 %.
    Asset-light models (software, cloud) generate high returns per
    dollar of assets.
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** Industry average ~0.5–1.5 %.
    Banks hold enormous asset bases (loans, securities), so even
    profitable banks show very low ROA. An ROA of 1 % is considered
    good for a bank — do not compare to tech benchmarks.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Industry average ~8–15 %.
    Fab plants are capital-intensive, pulling ROA down relative to
    pure software companies.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Industry average ~2–5 %.
    Heavy infrastructure assets (power plants, grid) depress ROA.
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Industry average ~3–8 %. Warehouses,
    fulfillment centers, and delivery fleets are asset-heavy.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** Industry average ~10–15 % for
    franchise models (MCD owns real estate but earns royalties).
  - **Bottom line:** ROA is most useful when comparing companies
    within the same sector. A bank's 1 % ROA and a tech firm's 20 %
    ROA can both be excellent.

### Return on Investment (ROI)
- **Definition:** Measures the gain or loss generated on an investment
  relative to the amount invested.
- **Formula:** (Gain from Investment − Cost of Investment) / Cost of
  Investment × 100
- **Interpretation:** Positive ROI means the investment is profitable.
  Compare across companies to assess relative investment efficiency.
- **Healthy range:** 10 %–25 %.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −20 % to +50 %.

## Income Statement Metrics

### Revenue
- **Definition:** Total income generated from the sale of goods or
  services before any expenses are deducted.  Also called "top line".
- **Interpretation:** Revenue growth signals expanding business, but must
  be evaluated alongside profitability metrics to confirm sustainability.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately $500 M–$400,000 M.

### Net Income
- **Definition:** The company's total profit after all expenses, taxes,
  interest, and depreciation have been subtracted from revenue.  Also
  called "bottom line".
- **Formula:** Revenue − All Expenses
- **Interpretation:** A positive net income means the company is
  profitable.  Negative net income indicates a loss.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$25,000 M to +$100,000 M.

### Gross Profit
- **Definition:** Revenue minus the direct costs of producing goods or
  services (cost of goods sold / COGS).
- **Formula:** Revenue − COGS
- **Interpretation:** High gross profit relative to revenue indicates
  strong pricing power or low production costs.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately $100 M–$170,000 M.

### EBITDA
- **Definition:** Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and
  Amortization.  A proxy for operating cash-flow profitability that
  strips out financing and accounting decisions.
- **Formula:** Net Income + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + Amortization
- **Interpretation:** Useful for comparing operational performance across
  companies with different capital structures.  Does not reflect actual
  cash generated because it excludes capital expenditures.
- **Healthy sign:** Positive and growing EBITDA.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$5,000 M to +$130,000 M.
- **Industry context — EBITDA margins (EBITDA/Revenue) by sector:**
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** EBITDA margins of ~30–45 %.
    Software has minimal COGS, so most revenue flows to EBITDA.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Margins of ~25–45 %.
    High during upcycles (NVDA AI boom), compressed during downturns
    (INTC competitive losses).
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Margins of ~5–15 %. Thin-margin
    business by design — high volume, heavy logistics costs. AMZN's
    EBITDA has grown mainly through AWS (cloud), which has much higher
    margins than retail.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** Margins of ~40–50 % for franchise
    models. Franchisees absorb operating costs; MCD collects royalties.
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** EBITDA is less meaningful for
    banks — interest income/expense is their core business, and EBITDA
    strips it out. Use net interest margin or net income instead.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Margins of ~15–25 %.
    Regulated pricing limits upside.

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)
- **Definition:** Net income allocated to each outstanding share of
  common stock.
- **Formula:** Net Income / Shares Outstanding
- **Interpretation:** Higher EPS signals greater profitability per share
  and is a key driver of stock price.
- **Dataset unit:** USD per share.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$30 to +$6.

## Balance Sheet & Liquidity Metrics

### Current Ratio
- **Definition:** Measures a company's ability to pay short-term
  obligations (due within one year) with short-term assets.
- **Formula:** Current Assets / Current Liabilities
- **Interpretation:**
  - **> 1.0** — the company can cover its short-term debts (healthy).
  - **< 1.0** — potential liquidity risk; may struggle to meet
    obligations.
  - **> 3.0** — possibly holding too much idle cash or inventory.
- **Dataset range:** approximately 0.5–4.0.
- **Industry context — why a current ratio below 1.0 is not always bad:**
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** Often run current ratios near or
    below 1.0 (industry average ~1.0–1.3). These companies have
    subscription-based or highly predictable revenue streams with short
    receivable cycles, so they don't need large current-asset buffers.
    They also aggressively return cash via buybacks, which reduces
    current assets. A CR of 0.7 for a large IT firm is common and not
    necessarily alarming.
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Typically runs a current ratio below
    1.0 (industry average ~0.9–1.1). Logistics and e-commerce companies
    collect from customers quickly (often at point of sale) but negotiate
    extended payment terms with suppliers, creating a negative working
    capital cycle that actually funds growth. A CR of 0.7 here is a
    feature of the business model, not a flaw.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** Often below 1.0 (industry average
    ~0.8–1.2). Fast-food chains have minimal receivables (cash/card
    sales), low inventory, and use franchise fees as predictable income.
    Heavy use of debt for buybacks also suppresses current assets.
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** Current ratio is less meaningful
    for banks because their balance sheets are structured differently —
    deposits are liabilities but are stable funding. Typical ratios are
    very low (< 0.5) but this reflects the nature of banking, not
    distress.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Tend to maintain higher
    current ratios (industry average ~1.5–2.5) because of long
    production cycles and large inventory buffers needed for chip
    fabrication. A CR of 0.7 in this sector would be genuinely
    concerning.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Moderate ratios (industry
    average ~1.0–1.5). Capital-intensive with regulated revenue, so they
    manage liquidity tightly. Below 1.0 may signal trouble here.
  - **FINTECH (PYPL):** Typically ~1.2–1.5. Digital payments companies
    hold customer funds as both assets and liabilities, inflating both
    sides. A low ratio could indicate regulatory reserve concerns.
  - **FINANCE (SHLDQ):** Similar to banks — low ratios are structural.
    However, SHLDQ's persistently low ratio alongside negative equity
    signals genuine distress, not just sector norms.
- **Bottom line:** Always compare a company's current ratio to its own
  sector average before judging it. A CR of 0.7 is normal in IT/LOGI/FOOD
  but would be a red flag in ELEC or MANUFACTURING.

### Debt/Equity Ratio
- **Definition:** Compares a company's total debt to its shareholder
  equity, indicating how much leverage the company uses.
- **Formula:** Total Debt / Shareholder Equity
- **Interpretation:**
  - **< 1.0** — more equity than debt; conservative financing.
  - **1.0–2.0** — moderate leverage; common in capital-intensive sectors.
  - **> 2.0** — high leverage; potentially risky if earnings decline.
  - **Negative** — shareholder equity is negative (accumulated losses
    exceed invested capital), a serious warning sign.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −10 to +30.
- **Industry context — why a D/E of 5 means different things by sector:**
  - **BANK / Insurance (AIG, BCS):** Banks routinely operate with D/E
    ratios of 5–10+ (industry average ~6–8). Their core business IS
    leverage — they take deposits (debt) and lend them out. A D/E of 5
    for a bank is normal and expected by regulators. What matters more
    for banks is the Tier 1 capital ratio, not D/E.
  - **MANUFACTURING / Utilities (PCG):** Capital-intensive businesses
    often carry D/E of 1.5–3.0 because they finance large
    infrastructure (power plants, pipelines) with long-term debt. A D/E
    of 5 would be high even for utilities and could signal over-leverage,
    especially if cash flows are regulated and predictable but thin.
  - **IT / SaaS (AAPL, GOOG, MSFT):** Industry average ~0.5–1.5.
    Tech companies tend to be asset-light with high margins, so they
    don't need much debt. Some (like AAPL) have taken on debt for tax-
    efficient buybacks despite having massive cash reserves, which can
    inflate D/E. A D/E of 5 for an IT company would be very unusual and
    concerning unless driven by buyback-related leverage with offsetting
    cash.
  - **LOGI / E-commerce (AMZN):** Moderate leverage (industry average
    ~1.0–2.5). E-commerce firms invest heavily in warehouses and
    logistics networks. A D/E of 5 would be elevated but context
    matters — Amazon's lease obligations inflate debt.
  - **FOOD / Restaurants (MCD):** Often have high D/E (industry average
    ~2–5+). McDonald's, for example, has had negative equity due to
    massive share buybacks funded by debt, resulting in extreme or
    negative D/E. For franchise-heavy models with predictable cash
    flows, high leverage is a deliberate financial strategy, not
    distress.
  - **ELEC / Semiconductors (INTC, NVDA):** Industry average ~0.3–1.0.
    Chip companies prefer equity financing due to cyclical revenues.
    A D/E of 5 would be a major red flag in this sector.
  - **FINTECH (PYPL):** Typically ~0.5–1.5. Light balance sheets. A D/E
    of 5 would be very concerning and could indicate aggressive
    acquisition debt or operational problems.
  - **FINANCE (SHLDQ):** Like banks, financial services firms use more
    leverage, but SHLDQ's negative equity makes D/E ratios misleading —
    focus on whether the company is solvent at all.
- **Bottom line:** A D/E of 5 is perfectly normal for a bank, elevated
  but explainable for food/restaurant franchises, and a serious warning
  sign for tech, semiconductor, or fintech companies. Always compare
  to the sector average and consider whether the leverage is structural
  (part of the business model) or a sign of financial strain.

### Shareholder Equity
- **Definition:** The residual interest in a company's assets after
  deducting liabilities.  Represents the net worth attributable to
  shareholders.
- **Formula:** Total Assets − Total Liabilities
- **Interpretation:** Positive and growing equity indicates the company
  is building value.  Negative equity is a red flag.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$15,000 M to +$270,000 M.

## Cash Flow Metrics

### Cash Flow from Operating Activities
- **Definition:** Cash generated or consumed by the company's core
  business operations.
- **Interpretation:** Positive operating cash flow means the business
  generates cash from its day-to-day activities, which is essential for
  sustainability.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$5,000 M to +$120,000 M.

### Cash Flow from Investing Activities
- **Definition:** Cash spent on or received from long-term investments
  such as property, equipment, or acquisitions.
- **Interpretation:** Typically negative (companies invest to grow).
  Large negative values may signal heavy capital expenditure; positive
  values may indicate asset sales.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$50,000 M to +$30,000 M.

### Cash Flow from Financial Activities
- **Definition:** Cash received from or paid to investors and creditors,
  including issuing stock, borrowing, repaying debt, and paying
  dividends.
- **Interpretation:** Positive values indicate the company raised
  capital; negative values indicate the company returned capital to
  shareholders (dividends, buybacks) or paid down debt.
- **Dataset unit:** millions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$120,000 M to +$30,000 M.

### Free Cash Flow per Share
- **Definition:** The cash remaining after capital expenditures,
  divided by shares outstanding.
- **Formula:** (Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures) / Shares
  Outstanding
- **Interpretation:** Positive free cash flow per share means the
  company generates surplus cash that can fund dividends, buybacks, or
  reinvestment.
- **Dataset unit:** USD per share.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −$5 to +$7.

## Market & Valuation Metrics

### Market Capitalisation (Market Cap)
- **Definition:** The total market value of a company's outstanding
  shares.
- **Formula:** Share Price × Shares Outstanding
- **Interpretation:**
  - **Large-cap (> $10 B)** — established, stable companies.
  - **Mid-cap ($2 B–$10 B)** — moderate growth potential.
  - **Small-cap (< $2 B)** — higher growth potential but higher risk.
- **Dataset unit:** billions USD.
- **Dataset range:** approximately $1 B–$3,000 B.

## Other Metrics

### Return on Tangible Equity
- **Definition:** Similar to ROE but excludes intangible assets
  (goodwill, patents) from equity, giving a stricter view of returns.
- **Formula:** Net Income / (Shareholder Equity − Intangible Assets)× 100
- **Interpretation:** More conservative than ROE.  Useful for comparing
  companies with significant intangible assets.
- **Dataset range:** approximately −200 % to +200 %.

### Number of Employees
- **Definition:** Total headcount of the company for the given fiscal
  year.
- **Interpretation:** Used alongside revenue or profit to calculate
  productivity ratios (e.g., revenue per employee).
- **Dataset range:** approximately 10,000–1,600,000.

### Inflation Rate (US)
- **Definition:** The annual percentage change in the US consumer price
  index (CPI), measuring how quickly prices are rising.
- **Interpretation:** High inflation erodes purchasing power and can
  squeeze profit margins.  Companies with pricing power can pass
  inflation costs to customers.
- **Dataset range:** approximately 0.1 %–8 %.

## How to Interpret Financial Health

A financially healthy company typically exhibits:

1. **Positive and growing revenue** — the business is expanding.
2. **Positive net profit margin** — the company keeps profit after costs.
3. **ROE between 15 %–25 %** — efficient use of shareholder capital.
4. **Current ratio > 1.0** — can meet short-term obligations.
5. **Debt/Equity ratio < 2.0** — not over-leveraged.
6. **Positive operating cash flow** — core operations generate cash.
7. **Positive free cash flow** — surplus cash after capital investments.

Warning signs include negative net income, current ratio below 1,
negative shareholder equity, and debt/equity ratio above 3.

**Important:** These thresholds are general guidelines. Always compare a
company's metrics to its own sector average before drawing conclusions.
Different industries have structurally different norms:
- Banks and financial firms carry high leverage by design (D/E of 5–10
  is normal) because their business model is built on borrowing and
  lending.
- SaaS/IT companies and e-commerce/logistics firms often run current
  ratios below 1.0 because they have predictable, recurring revenue
  streams and collect cash faster than they pay suppliers (negative
  working capital). This is a sign of business model efficiency, not
  distress.
- Fast-food franchises (like MCD) deliberately take on high debt for
  share buybacks because their franchise fee income is extremely
  predictable, making high leverage a calculated strategy.
- Capital-intensive sectors (utilities, semiconductors) need more
  liquidity buffers because their revenue can be cyclical and their
  assets are illiquid.
A metric that looks alarming in isolation may be perfectly healthy when
viewed in the context of the company's sector and business model.

## Cross-Metric Relationships

When interpreting financial metrics, reading them in combination is more
informative than looking at any single number:

- **High ROE + High D/E ratio:** The strong returns are being driven by
  leverage (debt), not operational excellence. This is riskier because a
  downturn could wipe out equity. Compare: AAPL may show ROE > 100 %
  because buybacks shrunk equity, while GOOG shows ~25 % ROE with low
  debt — GOOG's returns are more sustainable.
- **High ROE + Low D/E ratio:** Genuinely strong operational performance.
  The company earns high returns without relying on borrowed money.
- **Negative Net Income + Positive Operating Cash Flow:** The company is
  generating cash from operations but has non-cash charges (depreciation,
  write-downs) dragging down reported profit. Not necessarily alarming —
  check if cash flow is consistently positive.
- **Positive Net Income + Negative Operating Cash Flow:** The company
  reports profit on paper but isn't collecting cash — a red flag for
  earnings quality. Could indicate aggressive revenue recognition or
  growing uncollected receivables.
- **Low Current Ratio + Strong Free Cash Flow:** The company doesn't
  need a large current-asset buffer because it generates plenty of cash.
  Common for IT and LOGI companies.
- **High D/E + Strong Cash Flow Coverage:** Debt is manageable because
  the company generates enough cash to service it. Common for FOOD
  (franchise models) and BANK sectors.
- **Declining Revenue + Declining Margins + Rising D/E:** A dangerous
  combination indicating a shrinking business taking on more debt. This
  pattern appears in SHLDQ (Sears) in its final years.

## Company Context & Notable Outliers

Understanding why certain companies show extreme values in the dataset:

### SHLDQ (Sears Holdings)
- **Sector:** FINANCE (historically retail/department stores)
- **Key story:** Sears filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October 2018.
  The dataset captures its decline: revenue falling year-over-year,
  persistently negative net income, negative shareholder equity, and a
  meaningless (negative) D/E ratio. SHLDQ is the clearest example of
  financial distress in this dataset.
- **Why outlier values:** Negative equity means accumulated losses exceed
  all invested capital. Negative ROE with negative equity can produce a
  misleadingly positive ROE number — always check if equity is negative
  before interpreting ROE.

### AAPL (Apple)
- **Sector:** IT
- **Key story:** Apple has had periods of negative or very low
  shareholder equity despite being one of the most profitable companies
  in the world. This is because Apple aggressively repurchased shares
  using debt (taking advantage of low interest rates and tax-efficient
  offshore cash repatriation). The result is extremely high ROE/ROTE
  and elevated D/E that do NOT indicate distress — they reflect a
  deliberate capital return strategy backed by ~$60–100 B in cash
  reserves.

### MCD (McDonald's)
- **Sector:** FOOD
- **Key story:** Similar to Apple, McDonald's has had negative
  shareholder equity due to massive debt-funded share buybacks. Its
  franchise-heavy model generates extremely predictable cash flows
  (~$8–10 B/year operating cash flow), making high leverage a deliberate
  and manageable strategy. A negative D/E for MCD is not comparable to
  a negative D/E for SHLDQ.

### PCG (PG&E Corporation)
- **Sector:** MANUFACTURING (utilities)
- **Key story:** PG&E filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2019
  due to wildfire liabilities in California. The dataset may show
  anomalous values around 2019–2020. PG&E emerged from bankruptcy in
  2020 but with a transformed balance sheet.

### NVDA (NVIDIA)
- **Sector:** ELEC
- **Key story:** NVIDIA's financial metrics show dramatic improvement
  from ~2016 onward, driven by the GPU computing and AI boom. Revenue,
  margins, and ROE all inflected sharply upward. This makes historical
  comparisons within NVDA less meaningful — the company's business
  fundamentally changed in scale.

## Macroeconomic Events in the Dataset (2009–2023)

The dataset spans several major economic events that affect all companies:

### 2009–2010: Post-Global Financial Crisis Recovery
- The 2008 financial crisis devastated banks and financial firms. AIG
  required a government bailout. Data from 2009 shows depressed earnings,
  low market caps, and weak profitability across most sectors. Recovery
  was uneven — tech recovered fastest, banks took years.

### 2020: COVID-19 Pandemic
- Sharp revenue and earnings drops for many companies in 2020, especially
  in food service (MCD), travel-adjacent, and physical retail. However,
  tech companies (AAPL, MSFT, GOOG, AMZN) saw accelerated growth due to
  remote work, e-commerce, and cloud computing demand. AMZN and NVDA
  particularly benefited.
- If a company shows a dip in 2020, it is likely pandemic-related and
  does not necessarily indicate a structural problem.

### 2021–2022: Post-COVID Boom and Inflation Surge
- 2021 saw a strong recovery and stimulus-driven growth. 2022 brought
  the highest US inflation in 40 years (~8 %), which squeezed margins
  for companies without pricing power. The Federal Reserve raised
  interest rates aggressively, increasing debt costs and depressing
  market caps, particularly for growth stocks.
- Companies with strong pricing power (AAPL, MSFT, MCD) weathered
  inflation better than those in competitive or regulated markets.

### 2023: Normalization and AI Boom
- Inflation moderated, markets recovered, and NVDA's revenue exploded
  due to AI chip demand. Tech sector market caps reached new highs.
  Banks remained cautious after the 2023 regional banking stress.
